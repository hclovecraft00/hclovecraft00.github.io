Become Cyclable

Meditations on life, Hokkaido, cycling, Singapore

21 August 2025 #article

It has been over a year since I took part in NUS-UTokyo Hokkaido Summer Program. The trip came after a period of narrative weirding in my personal life — I’I had just completed my internship at the Centre for Strategic Futures and started organising with Post-growth Singapore — very significant experiences to say the least. My stay in rural Hokkaido was a salve. I still remember the vastness of the skies and roads, how it got so dark we could observe constellations and galaxies in the sky. I left Hokkaido much less afraid of bugs, much more willing to eat wild plants, and with a deep love for cycling.

We stayed in Memu Earth Labs, an experimental compound in Taiki Town mainly for engineers/architects to prototype experimental infrastructure. It was a massive area with fields stretching far into the horizon and interesting architectural projects dotted around the compound. We spent most of our time in a barn-like hall where we worked on our end-trip project. There weren’t any public buses available, and with the nearest town being a 2-hour walk away, we naturally took to the bicycle as our main form of transportation.

The roads were smooth and grid-like, typically used only by large trucks carrying agricultural cargo. Scattered across the countryside were these desire paths that led into the forests, traced to the sea, and guided us to shrines — all paths that were not easily accessible via car. The bicycle was shaping my experience of the environment. While this is a no-brainer, it’s hard to overstate how impactful it is to travel fully embodied in the local environment. In cars you’re stationary, air conditioned, isolated from the world. On a bike you’re attuned to the breeze, texture and bumps of the road, even smells (there were a lot of dairy cow farms around us). With all senses engaged, the feeling is salient — I am not a cartesian observer, but an agent in an ongoing negotiation between rider, machine, environment, intent, effect, consequence.

The Absent Body (Leder, 1990)

This heightened awareness of my corporeal body was significant. Phenomenology is a field concerned with how we experience the world as conscious, embodied beings. While I can only speak for myself, I often find my sensing of my body absent. As I sit on this lawn chair typing away, I can feel the ache in my neck and shoulder from repeated bad posture, I can feel the grass brushing against my soles, I can’t feel my calf, or bicep, or waist. I know they’re there; I just can’t sense it without conscious effort to engage them. My body becomes present in times of disruption, dysfunction, and effort — cycling.

Motor Intentionality

Coined by French philosopher Maurice Merleau-Ponty, motor intentionality is a form of bodily agency — an “integrated suite of skills, capacities, and habits—not all of which are available to consciousness—that enable this unreflective action” (Rietveld 2008). You don’t consciously and meticulously measure yourself against every door to deem your ability to walk through it. Perhaps you’re carrying something pointy and large in your bag, and you feel this odd extension of perception to said temporary, tangible object leading to you turning corners in a unnatural way for fear of damaging the jutting object. This phenomenon is especially acute when I find myself cycling to West Coast Park where I feel this cramping and squeezing around my elbows passing through a particular stretch of pedestrian path next to the highway. It’s a queer feeling as, technically speaking, it is feasible for me to pass through. Yet somehow, I am able to sense the margin of error, the wobbliness and space I need as a novice cyclist. My perception autonomously deemed that path un-cyclable.

A bicycle, at the end of the day, is a technology, a tool one uses to get from A to B, and a tool is something one can master, and which one can find an extension of himself.

Singapore

If it is not obvious enough, I like cycling. The intentionality, the agency, the flow state. To put it reductively, it’s fun. But cycling in Singapore is not easy temperature-wise, safety-wise, infrastructure-wise, everything-wise. Cycling on the roads are tough as it seems as if every car is out to hit and kill you. Cycling on (government constructed) paths requires dexterity in weaving between pedestrians who decide to walk onto the cycling path for some reason. Furthermore, these cycling “paths” aren’t much of paths at all! Ignoring best practices as demonstrated across the world — they’re just regular pedestrian roads painted a deeper shade of red.

Its upsetting, really, that such a small country grew to be so car reliant. Yes while Singapore does have a great (but not necessarily reliable) public transport system, it is simply insufficient when looked at relative to our population (yuuka, 2023). On internet forums one can find many expressions of disdain towards cyclists and cycling, and many more renditions of the same “we should, but we just can’t do it because ___” mantra.

We should cycle even if it is hard. We should cycle because it is hard. Not out of masochistic impulse but because the experience can be so meaningful and honest. Cycling helped me become conscious of my own energy expenditure (and carbon footprint) through the felt experience of pedaling against headwind or up an incline. It helped me explore communities and neighborhoods I would have otherwise not gone into. It helped me get to classes on time. It helped me enjoy the journey!

Let’s cycle, Singapore.
