Why Study Economics?

Is it eeek-onomics or echoe-nomics?

16 September 2025 #article

It’s the second most asked question I get, right after “are you Singaporean?”/”Where’s your accent from?”. I don’t mind it, as each iteration teases out something new, something insightful. I like to think that I am finally ready to put this question to rest and to writing, once and for all.

Q: Why did you choose to study economics?

A: Great question! I was never actually good at economics in JC. I didn’t like it for all the reasons most don’t — it just didn’t make sense to reduce a human person into a set of preferences wrapped up in a utility function. One day, in conversations with my now-ex, I brought up the idea of minimum wage. His retort was quick and snappy: “It’ll just lead to price inflation and why would employers hire someone expensive?”.

His argument wasn’t necessarily wrong — empirical evidence is conflicting and highly contextual. Rather, it was the knee-jerk, immediate response that stood out to me. He said it like it was common-sense, as if I was stupid to suggest otherwise.

I think that’s when it really set in just how ingrained the neoliberal “homo-economics” mental model was, and I figured if we could find a way to articulate a different economics — one turned towards positive/different values — perhaps we could orient people to similar ends, expanding the definition of what is possible while incorporating what is right. Put simply, how do we reformulate the Kool-Aid?

That’s why I quite like Doughnut Economics. While traditional economic models resemble an infinite canvas with some variable X and Y, the Doughnut instead imposes a strict upper bound to reflect natural, biological limits of the Earth/region, and a lower bound representative of minimum amount economic activity to ensure everyone’s access to basic human needs.

**Q: How would something like that be implemented? **

A: Admittedly, we don’t know! It’s definitely not as easy as just letting the “free market” do its thing. It’ll take a lot of work to operationalise and we’ll have to come up with a slew of new metrics and KPIs to ensure we’re on track too. But isn’t that the point? Any fool can point at GDP and recognise it as “inconclusive”, let’s be the fools that try and do something better fitted for human and ecological wellbeing, beyond our standard conceptions of growth.

The purpose of a system is what it does, yada yada.

Q: Isn’t that too different from Economics then? Seems more like a policy issue for the political sciences/sociologists/anthropologists/…

A: Economics has become increasingly interdisciplinary, expanding into law, behavioural science and even emerging technologies like crypto. The field is fundamentally about how to allocate resources efficiently. We can debate what 1) allocate, 2) resources, and 3) efficient means. It just so happens our current system conceives of it as 1) market mechanisms, 2) global comparative advantage, and 3) minimal state intervention. Economics shapes how we see the world functioning and if we want a different world, we need different economics. None of our processes are wholly “natural”, there is no linear map of human progression.

Q: So where is your accent from?

A: Ask me in person.
